# AI-associated writing mannerisms

Last researched: not yet

This is a revision catalog, not an authorship test. Many listed patterns predate generative AI and are perfectly natural in moderation. Flag clusters, repetition, and poor fit with the genre—not isolated words.

## Evidence labels

- `supported`: backed by replicated or methodologically transparent evidence.
- `tentative`: plausible observation that needs stronger or broader evidence.
- `preference`: a house-style choice, not a general AI association.

The initial entries below are working heuristics and remain `tentative` until the scheduled research process evaluates sources.

## Catalog

### Canned scene-setting

Status: tentative

Signals include broad openings such as “in today's fast-paced world,” “in an ever-evolving landscape,” and “as we navigate.” They delay the actual claim and could introduce almost any topic.

Revision: begin with the concrete subject, event, tension, or claim. Keep contextual framing only when it changes how the reader should understand the point.

Counterexample: a historical or policy analysis may need a short statement of changed conditions.

### Inflated significance without evidence

Status: tentative

Signals include calling routine facts “pivotal,” “profound,” “groundbreaking,” a “testament,” or evidence of a broad “shift” without showing why. The prose performs importance instead of establishing it.

Revision: state the measurable consequence, affected group, comparison, or causal link.

Counterexample: significance language is appropriate when the draft immediately supports it.

### Abstract metaphor clusters

Status: tentative

Signals include repeated use of “landscape,” “realm,” “tapestry,” “journey,” “beacon,” “cornerstone,” “unlock,” and “delve.” The concern is density and vagueness, not any single word.

Revision: name the actual system, field, process, constraint, or action.

Counterexample: an intentional metaphor can clarify a difficult concept or establish a literary voice.

### Canned transitions and throat-clearing

Status: tentative

Signals include frequent “moreover,” “furthermore,” “additionally,” “it is important to note,” “it is worth noting,” and “with that in mind.” These can make relationships between ideas feel assembled rather than reasoned.

Revision: remove the transition when sequence is already clear, or name the relationship directly: cause, contrast, exception, consequence, or example.

Counterexample: formal, legal, and academic genres often require explicit transitions.

### Formulaic contrast

Status: tentative

Signals include repeated “not only X, but also Y,” “it's not just X; it's Y,” and “rather than X, Y.” Repetition creates an overly balanced, promotional cadence.

Revision: state the stronger proposition directly or use an asymmetrical sentence that reflects the actual relationship.

Counterexample: contrast is useful when correcting a genuine misconception.

### Repetitive triplets and parallel lists

Status: tentative

Signals include serial three-part lists, multiple sentences with the same grammatical frame, and neat sets of three whose members overlap.

Revision: keep only distinct items, combine overlaps, and vary structure when the rhythm has become predictable.

Counterexample: parallelism and the rule of three are established rhetorical tools.

### Excessive sectioning and recap

Status: tentative

Signals include headings for very short passages, mini-conclusions after each section, and a final conclusion that merely repeats the introduction.

Revision: let adjacent ideas flow together and use the ending to supply consequence, decision, limitation, or next step.

Counterexample: scannable reference material, accessibility-oriented instructions, and long technical documents benefit from explicit structure.

### Uniformly polished sentence rhythm

Status: tentative

Signals include similar sentence lengths, repeated topic-sentence-plus-explanation paragraphs, and consistently complete transitions with no change in pace.

Revision: vary emphasis according to the content. Use a short sentence when the point deserves one; combine closely related ideas when separation feels mechanical.

Counterexample: controlled language and regulated documentation may intentionally favor uniform syntax.

### Generic reader address and universalizing

Status: tentative

Signals include “whether you're a beginner or an expert,” “we all know,” “everyone can,” and generic “you” statements that assume motives or experience.

Revision: identify the actual audience or describe the condition without inventing consensus.

Counterexample: inclusive reader address can suit onboarding, consumer guidance, and conversational instruction.

### Unsupported reassurance or certainty

Status: tentative

Signals include “rest assured,” “the good news is,” “seamlessly,” “effortlessly,” “undoubtedly,” and confident predictions unsupported by the available evidence.

Revision: state the relevant evidence, limitation, or practical condition. Preserve uncertainty where it matters.

Counterexample: reassurance may be appropriate when it follows a demonstrated safety or support guarantee.

### Generic concluding uplift

Status: tentative

Signals include endings such as “the future is bright,” “the possibilities are endless,” “embrace the journey,” or a broad call to action unrelated to a specific decision.

Revision: end with the implication, recommendation, unresolved question, or concrete next action.

Counterexample: speeches and campaign copy may intentionally close with aspiration.
